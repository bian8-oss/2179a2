Set Aside

Australia has protected an area larger than Germany, France and Spain combined.
This is the story of where, who, and whether it's enough.

By J. Bian · May 2026
01

The Big Picture

It's one of the world's great conservation experiments. More than a fifth of the continent has been formally set aside for nature — but the headline number hides more than it reveals.

Australia is a country of extremes. It is the driest inhabited continent, the flattest, and one of the most biologically distinct. Over a century and a half, governments, communities, and Traditional Owners have placed more than 22% of the landmass under some form of protection — an area roughly the size of Western Europe.

02

The Paradox

22% of the country is protected. But of Australia's 89 bioregions, dozens fall below the international target. We've protected a lot — just not always the right places.

Quantity is not quality. A bioregion is a distinctive ecological zone: the tropical savannas of Arnhem Land, the temperate forests of Tasmania, the deserts of the Centre. International guidance recommends that at least 17% of every bioregion be safeguarded. Many of Australia's are nowhere close.

03

The Quiet Revolution

In 1998, Australia did something no other country had done at scale. It handed land back. Today, Indigenous Protected Areas make up nearly half of the National Reserve System — and most Australians have never heard of them.

The first Indigenous Protected Area (IPA) was declared in 1998 over Nantawarrina in South Australia — 58,000 hectares of land cared for by the Adnyamathanha people. The model was simple: recognise, in law, what Traditional Owners had been doing for tens of thousands of years. Today, more than 85 million hectares are managed under IPA agreements, spanning deserts, wetlands, and sea country across northern and central Australia.

04

A Century of Protection

From a single park in 1879 to more than 14,000 today. The story of Australia's protected areas is the story of changing values.

Royal National Park, established south of Sydney in 1879, was the world's second national park — six years after Yellowstone. For decades, "protection" meant scenery for visitors. By the 1970s it meant biodiversity. By the 1990s, Country. The map below is the cumulative result of 145 years of shifting priorities.

05

Victoria's Paradox

Victoria is home to a quarter of all Australians — but accounts for just a sliver of the country's protected estate. We live next to one of the system's biggest gaps.

Look closely at the map of Victoria and a different story emerges. The state has been settled, farmed, and urbanised more thoroughly than any other. What remains is fragmented — pockets of alpine country in the east, Mallee scrub in the north-west, a thin coastal belt. For Victorians, the question isn't how much more, but what we still have.

06

Looking Forward

Every nation has pledged to protect 30% of its land and sea by 2030. Australia is closer than most. But the gap is not measured in hectares — it is measured in which hectares.

The 30 by 30 target, adopted at the 2022 UN Biodiversity Conference, is now the global benchmark. Australia's chances of hitting it are good — on paper. Whether the country meets it in spirit, by closing the gaps in bioregion coverage and species range, is a question for the next six years.

The country has set aside a fifth of itself. The next chapter is choosing the right fifth.
